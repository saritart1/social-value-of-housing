Social value against rent

Definitions and sources: what these numbers are (and are not)

Definitions

Price

The annual market rent of one housing unit, dollars per unit per year. Owner-occupied units are valued at imputed rent. This is a yearly flow, not the sale price of a house; multiply by roughly 20 for an asset-value equivalent (the percentages do not change).

Social value

The yearly value to everyone in the metro of one additional unit at that location: the occupant’s own value plus everything that lands on other people.

Wedge

Social value minus price: the part the market price misses. It contains only effects on other people; scarcity and the occupant’s own benefit are already in the price. Rent changes are transfers between renters and landlords, so they are distribution, not social value.

Channels

Agglomeration (λ): the new resident makes others more productive at the workplace. Traffic (δc): their commuting slows others down (negative) or, where building shortens commutes, relieves congestion (positive). Density amenity (ω): more neighbors change what the place is like for the people already there.

Channel interactions

Each channel is measured by switching it off one at a time; because the channels interact in equilibrium, a small remainder does not attribute cleanly to any single one and is shown separately. Not to be confused with the ξ/Ā residuals below, which are baseline level objects and never enter the wedge.

Rank

Position of the PUMA within its metro when sorted by the wedge.

Captured by the price

Price divided by social value. Below 100%: the price understates the unit’s social value (positive wedge). Above 100%: the price overstates it (negative wedge; the unit’s spillovers on others are net negative).

Rent–supply elasticity (η)

How far rent at a location falls when its housing stock rises by one percent. It is never an input: every elasticity is borrowed from elsewhere and the location levels are inverted on populations and wages, so the model’s own value is free to be wrong. Rollet (2025) estimates −0.42 causally off New York upzonings.

The residuals (ξ and Ā)

Everything the model does not specify about a place is absorbed by two location-specific residuals, recovered by inversion: Ā (each type’s productivity at each workplace) is backed out so model wages equal observed wages exactly, and ξ (each type’s preference for each location: perceived schools, coastline, history) is backed out so the model’s population map equals the observed one exactly. They are fitted to the data, never to the results, and held fixed in every counterfactual. Re-inverting ξ on data from six years earlier recovers nearly the same values (correlation 0.97 or above in every metro). The small drift tracks density growth, which is one of the checks behind the density-amenity calibration. In the memo the commuting cost is κ and the residual is ξ.

Ability types

What a type is

In the model a type is a fixed trait, carried with you when you move. It sets how much you earn at each workplace and how much you gain from working near others.

Why not income

Income cannot serve: it is an outcome of where you live and work, so sorting households by income defines the type using something the model itself determines.

How ability is measured

Ability is not observed, so it is proxied. Workers are ranked by their predicted earnings, and six equal groups of that ranking are the types. The prediction uses only traits settled before anyone picks a neighbourhood: education, age, sex, field of degree, where someone was born and when they arrived, English proficiency, and race. The wage equation carries a fixed effect for every PUMA, so the coefficients are within-location returns and the index holds no information about where a person lives.

Why not education alone

Education is exogenous but blunt. Six education groups reproduce 40–46% of the earnings gap between rich and poor neighbourhoods, depending on the metro. The index reproduces 54–63%. Education is the largest single input to it, not a rival to it.

What it cannot reach

Between a third and a half of the neighbourhood earnings gap is still not reproduced. That residual is the sorting on unobservables the model exists to explain, so it should not be recoverable from observed traits. Occupation and industry would raise the share further but are themselves plausibly chosen alongside a neighbourhood, so they are held back as a sensitivity rather than used here.

Data

Geography

2020 Census PUMAs (areas of ~100,000+ residents), six metros, 409 PUMAs.

Rents, housing stock, incomes

American Community Survey, 5-year 2018–2022.

Wages and worker traits

ACS PUMS person records, 2022 (2022 dollars), by ability type and workplace.

Commuting flows

LODES 2022 (Census employer-employee blocks); the model matches observed flows exactly.

Travel times

Free-flow driving times on the OpenStreetMap road network.

Behavioral parameters

Borrowed from published causal studies (Rollet 2025; Diamond 2016; Bryan–Morten 2019; Ciccone–Hall 1996; Combes–Duranton–Gobillon 2008; Ahlfeldt et al. 2015), never estimated here. Location levels are inverted so the model reproduces observed populations and wages exactly.

Validation

The model reproduces the causal rent-supply elasticity of −0.42 (Rollet 2025, NYC upzonings) in all six metros with no tuning.
